Executive Summary
Farmers in Chhattisgarh who wish to purchase agricultural inputs online face a compounded barrier:
conventional e-commerce interfaces are text-heavy, assume digital literacy, and operate in Hindi or
English rather than Chhattisgarhi, an Eastern Hindi Indo-Aryan language spoken by more than
sixteen million people that has its own vocabulary, pronouns and verb forms. This proposal
describes a B.Tech Minor Project that builds and evaluates a voice-first Chhattisgarhi shopping
assistant restricted to a single, well-defined workflow: product discovery, price and quantity queries,
and conversational cart management for a curated agricultural product catalogue.

The proposed academic deliverable is deliberately narrow. It is not a payments-enabled
marketplace, not a multi-vendor platform, and not an autonomous agronomic advisor. It is a working
mobile prototype in which a farmer can speak in Chhattisgarhi, have the system correctly recognise
the request, retrieve or calculate the relevant product information from a structured database, and
hear a Chhattisgarhi voice response, while every diagnostic or dosage-related agricultural question
is deliberately redirected to a human expert at the local Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) rather than
answered by the AI.

The team has already built and locally run a working Chhattisgarhi speech-to-speech pipeline — a
FastAPI-based streaming speech-to-text service using Meta's MMS (Massively Multilingual Speech)
model with a Chhattisgarhi (hne) adapter, and a Coqui-TTS VITS-based Chhattisgarhi speech
synthesiser with trained male and female voice checkpoints. This preliminary work is used in this
proposal as evidence that the core voice-interaction component is technically demonstrated, and it
is deliberately reused as the primary technical foundation of the Minor Project rather than being
replaced by unconfirmed third-party APIs.

Because the project currently has no allocated budget and no confirmed access to the Bhashini
government API or to any paid large-language-model (LLM) service, the technology strategy
prioritises free, open-source, and locally runnable components wherever an academically defensible
open alternative exists, and explicitly marks any component that would depend on paid or
unconfirmed infrastructure as a future, institutionally-approved possibility rather than a current
deliverable.

The Minor Project's research contribution is framed around evaluating a cascaded, low-resource-
language conversational pipeline for agricultural commerce — specifically, how accurately
Chhattisgarhi speech can be converted into structured, safety-bounded shopping actions, and what
accuracy, latency, and reliability trade-offs this cascaded architecture exhibits. The proposal also

1. Background and Motivation
Smartphone penetration in rural Chhattisgarh has increased substantially over the past decade, yet
the interfaces that mediate access to agricultural commerce and information have not kept pace
with the linguistic and literacy realities of their intended users. Government portals such as e-NAM
and Kisan Suvidha, and private agri-marketplaces such as DeHaat, are built around Hindi- or
English-language, text-and-menu navigation. For a smallholder farmer with limited formal education,
typing agricultural terminology on a small touchscreen keyboard is slow and error-prone, and the
underlying language of the interface is frequently not the farmer's first language.

Chhattisgarhi is grammatically and lexically distinct from standard Hindi. It uses its own pronouns
(for example, “mola” for “to me” and “tor” for “yours”) and its own verb conjugation patterns, and a
large share of its speakers use it in preference to Hindi in everyday conversation, particularly in
rural and semi-urban areas. Voice assistants and speech interfaces built for standard Hindi routinely
mis-transcribe Chhattisgarhi speech, and this friction pushes users away from otherwise-useful
digital services.

Recent developments in Indic speech and language technology are changing what is feasible for a
low-resource language such as Chhattisgarhi. Meta's Massively Multilingual Speech (MMS) project
released acoustic models with adapters for over a thousand languages, including a Chhattisgarhi
(‘hne’) adapter, which the team has already used locally. IISc's SPIRE Lab, in partnership with
ARTPARK, has released large open Chhattisgarhi speech resources through the Project Vaani,
RESPIN and SYSPIN initiatives, and — most recently, in August 2026 — an open-source multilingual
ASR model named SraVaani-1.0 that explicitly includes low-resource Indian dialects. Meta's
NLLB-200 translation model natively supports Chhattisgarhi under the FLORES-200 code hne_Deva.
These developments mean that, for the first time, a student team can realistically assemble a
Chhattisgarhi voice pipeline predominantly from free and open resources rather than depending on
a single commercial vendor.

This Minor Project sits at the intersection of three concerns that the team considers individually
well understood but rarely combined in a single, rigorously scoped student project: (a) low-resource
speech and language processing for an under-served Indian dialect, (b) safety-constrained

conversational AI for a domain — agriculture — where incorrect automated advice carries real-world
consequences, and (c) a transactional, stateful e-commerce backend. The proposal that follows is
written to keep these three concerns in careful balance rather than allowing any one of them
(particularly the AI) to be oversold.


2. Problem Statement
Digitally and linguistically marginalised farmers in Chhattisgarh are effectively excluded from
convenient online purchase of agricultural inputs such as seed, fertiliser and basic tools, because
the dominant e-commerce and agri-tech interfaces available to them (i) are optimised for text entry
rather than speech, (ii) are built for Hindi or English rather than Chhattisgarhi, and (iii) provide no
mechanism for a user to converse naturally about what they need, how much of it they need for a
given field size, and whether it is available and at what price.

At the same time, any voice-based agricultural assistant carries a distinct hazard that a generic e-
commerce voice assistant does not: if the assistant is allowed to behave as an open-ended
conversational agent, it may be asked diagnostic or dosage questions (“which pesticide should I
use for this disease?”) that a large language model can answer fluently but incorrectly, with
consequences ranging from crop loss to health and environmental harm. A system that is voice-
enabled but not safety-bounded is therefore not an acceptable design for this domain.

The problem this Minor Project addresses is consequently twofold and inseparable: (1) building a
voice interface that lets a Chhattisgarhi-speaking farmer discover agricultural products and manage
a shopping cart by speech alone, with acceptable accuracy and latency on a constrained student
budget, and (2) constraining that same interface so that it functions strictly as a retail/discovery
assistant and never as an autonomous source of agronomic diagnosis or dosage advice.


3. Rationale
The rationale for restricting the academic deliverable to voice-first product discovery and cart
management, rather than attempting a full agricultural marketplace, follows directly from a
feasibility review of what a three-member B.Tech team can responsibly deliver, test, and defend
within a single semester-scale (approximately sixteen-week) window.

  • A full multi-vendor marketplace with live inventory synchronisation, real payments, and
     logistics is a multi-year engineering effort even for a funded startup team, and attempting it
     inside a Minor Project would guarantee an unfinished, unevaluable deliverable.




                     Dr. SPM International Institute of Information and Technology • Page 4 of 33
                 Chhattisgarhi Voice-Based Agricultural Shopping Assistant — B.Tech Minor Project Proposal



  • The team's own preliminary work already demonstrates that the hardest novel component —
     real-time Chhattisgarhi speech recognition and synthesis on local, open-source models — is
     achievable, which materially de-risks the voice layer of the project relative to the e-commerce
     layer.

  • A narrow, well-instrumented scope (search, cart, price/quantity queries, simulated checkout)
     allows the team to collect meaningful, honestly-reported evaluation metrics (Word Error Rate,
     intent accuracy, task completion rate) instead of producing an impressive-looking but
     unmeasured system.

  • Restricting the AI to a retail-assistant role, with hardcoded safety refusals for diagnostic
     agronomic queries, keeps the academic prototype within a defensible risk envelope and
     avoids the liability associated with automated pesticide or dosage recommendations.

Building the technically rigorous, safety-bounded MVP first, and treating commercialisation-oriented
features (payments, vendor onboarding, logistics, broader regional-language support) as an explicit
future-scope discussion rather than a current commitment, is the approach recommended by the
team's own feasibility research and is retained as the guiding principle of this proposal.

5. Research Gap
Prior computational-linguistics work on Chhattisgarhi has largely treated speech recognition and
translation as isolated benchmarking tasks — for instance, the RESPIN corpus and associated
baseline ASR models evaluate word error rate on read speech, and NLLB-200 is evaluated on
general-domain sentence translation. There is comparatively little published work that studies a
multi-turn, state-aware, transactional agent that must correctly extract compound agricultural
entities (crop variety, area, unit, quantity) from informal, code-switched Chhattisgarhi speech and
preserve that state correctly across an e-commerce cart workflow.

The academic contribution of this Minor Project is therefore not the existence of an agricultural
shopping application, nor the existence of Chhattisgarhi ASR or TTS in isolation — both already
exist in various forms — but the integration and empirical evaluation of a cascaded, low-resource-
language voice pipeline (ASR → translation → constrained LLM tool-calling → deterministic


                    Dr. SPM International Institute of Information and Technology • Page 6 of 33
              Chhattisgarhi Voice-Based Agricultural Shopping Assistant — B.Tech Minor Project Proposal



backend → translation → TTS) as applied to a transactional agricultural-commerce state machine,
together with an explicit, tested safety boundary that separates permitted shopping queries from
disallowed agronomic-diagnostic queries.


6. Research Questions
The project is organised around the following research questions, each of which has a
corresponding evaluation metric described in Section 24 (Evaluation Methodology):

  1. RQ1: How accurately can spontaneous, informal Chhattisgarhi speech be converted into
     correctly structured shopping intents and entities (product, crop, quantity, unit) using a
     cascaded ASR → translation → LLM pipeline built primarily from open-source components?

  2. RQ2: How does each stage of the cascade (ASR transcription error, machine-translation drift,
     LLM entity-extraction error) individually contribute to end-to-end intent-and-entity accuracy?

  3. RQ3: Can a constrained, tool-calling LLM architecture reliably convert informal Chhattisgarhi
     farmer commands into valid, schema-conformant e-commerce operations (search, add-to-
     cart, remove-from-cart, view-cart) without hallucinating non-existent products or unsafe
     agronomic advice?

  4. RQ4: What is the practical latency of a locally-hosted, zero-budget voice pipeline compared
     to an API-assisted alternative, and what latency is acceptable to a first-time voice-commerce
     user?

  5. RQ5: Is a voice-first interface measurably more usable, for the target persona of a low-
     digital-literacy Chhattisgarhi-speaking farmer, than a conventional text-based product search
     interface for the same catalogue?

8. Target Users
The system is designed around three user personas, adapted from the team's feasibility research,
representing the range of digital-literacy and connectivity conditions expected among Chhattisgarhi-
speaking farmers in and around Chhattisgarh, and the adjoining Chhattisgarhi-speaking regions of
Madhya Pradesh and Odisha.

Persona 1: Smallholder farmer with limited digital literacy
Farms one to three acres, relies on rainfed agriculture, speaks primarily Chhattisgarhi, and finds
standard grid-and-menu e-commerce apps difficult to navigate. This user depends on the voice
assistant for essentially the entire shopping interaction and needs the interface to be forgiving of
informal, hesitant, or incomplete spoken commands.

Persona 2: Medium-scale farmer with intermediate digital literacy
Manages upwards of five acres, frequently code-switches between Hindi and Chhattisgarhi, is
comfortable with basic smartphone navigation, but prefers voice for quickly checking product
availability or calculating input quantities for a given area rather than typing and scrolling.

Persona 3: Intermittent-connectivity user
Operates in a rural zone with fluctuating 3G/4G connectivity. This persona motivates the
requirement, described in Section 19, that the mobile client degrade gracefully — with clear
feedback and local caching — rather than fail silently when a voice request times out.


9. Proposed Solution
The proposed solution is a Chhattisgarhi voice-first agricultural product discovery and shopping-cart
assistant, delivered as a lightweight mobile application backed by a REST API and a relational
database. A farmer speaks a request in Chhattisgarhi; the system transcribes it, extracts the
shopping intent and any product/quantity/area entities, executes the corresponding deterministic
backend operation, and replies with a spoken Chhattisgarhi response. The core interaction loop is
summarised below.

9.1 Conceptual interaction flow
  • Farmer speaks a request in Chhattisgarhi (e.g. a search, a quantity question, or an
     add/remove-from-cart command).

  • On-device voice activity detection (VAD) identifies the utterance boundary and streams
     compressed audio to the backend.

  • A Chhattisgarhi automatic speech recognition (ASR) stage transcribes the audio to
     Devanagari text.

  • A machine-translation stage normalises the Chhattisgarhi text into English (necessary
     because current LLMs reason far more reliably in English than in a low-resource language, as
     discussed in Section 13.3).

  • A constrained, tool-calling LLM classifies the intent, extracts entities, and — if the query is a
     disallowed agronomic-diagnostic question — triggers a safety refusal instead of a database
     operation.

  • For a permitted shopping intent, the backend performs the corresponding deterministic
     operation (search, quantity calculation from verified product metadata, cart update) against
     the PostgreSQL product/cart database.

  • The result is translated back into Chhattisgarhi and rendered as speech through the TTS
     stage, and streamed back to the mobile client.

This cascaded architecture is chosen over a hypothetical end-to-end speech-to-speech model

10. System Scope: Three Levels of Maturity
To avoid any ambiguity about what is being promised in this Minor Project, the proposal explicitly
separates the work into three levels. This separation is maintained consistently through every later
section of the document.

Level 1 — Already Demonstrated (Preliminary Work)
A working, locally-run Chhattisgarhi speech-to-speech pipeline already exists, built by the team prior
to this proposal. It performs real-time streaming ASR using Meta's MMS model with a Chhattisgarhi
adapter, and speech synthesis using a Coqui-TTS VITS model with trained male and female
Chhattisgarhi voice checkpoints. Full technical detail is given in Section 30 (Preliminary Work). This
is evidence of feasibility for the voice-interaction component only — it does not include e-commerce,
agricultural entities, or safety-boundary logic, all of which are new work for this Minor Project.

Level 2 — Minor Project MVP (This Proposal's Deliverable)
The system to be designed, built and evaluated between now and approximately mid-December
2026: Chhattisgarhi voice input for product search, product details, deterministic quantity
calculation, conversational add-to-cart and remove-from-cart, cart viewing, price checking, and a
simulated (non-payment) checkout, delivered through a basic Flutter mobile application and a
FastAPI backend, with the safety boundary described in Section 18 enforced throughout.

Level 3 — Future Startup / Product Direction (Explicitly Out of Scope Now)
A possible longer-term evolution of the validated MVP into a broader regional-language agricultural
commerce platform, potentially including real UPI/payment integration, multi-vendor inventory,
logistics and delivery tracking, farmer profiles, additional regional languages, and safety-controlled
personalised recommendations. Level 3 is discussed only in Section 29 (Startup and Future
Expansion) as a future possibility contingent on institutional support and is not claimed as part of
the current academic deliverable.

13.3 Why a translation step is used
Contemporary LLMs exhibit markedly weaker reasoning, tool-calling reliability, and context retention
when prompted directly in a low-resource language such as Chhattisgarhi, compared with English,
because their pretraining data is overwhelmingly English-dominant. Routing the transcribed
Chhattisgarhi text through a Chhattisgarhi–English translation stage before LLM reasoning is
therefore treated as a necessary architectural choice rather than an optional one, and the
translation stage's own error contribution is measured separately as part of RQ2.

13.4 Complete architecture flow
The mobile client records audio, applies local VAD, and streams compressed (Opus-codec) audio
to the FastAPI gateway. The gateway dispatches the audio to the ASR stage, passes the resulting
Chhattisgarhi text through the MT stage, and sends the English text — together with a system
prompt defining the permitted tool schema and the safety boundary — to the LLM. When the LLM
emits a tool call (for example add_to_cart), the backend executes the corresponding SQL
transaction against PostgreSQL, returns a structured result to the LLM for response generation,
translates the English response back to Chhattisgarhi, synthesises audio via TTS, and streams the
response back to the client.

14. Agricultural Product Data Model and Database Architecture
14.1 Rationale for a deterministic, rule-based recommendation layer
A central design principle of this project, carried directly from the team's feasibility research, is that
agricultural quantity recommendations must never be produced from an LLM's internal knowledge.
The LLM's role is strictly limited to natural-language routing (understanding what the farmer is
asking for); any quantity calculation is performed by ordinary, auditable Python/SQL logic operating
on verified product metadata (for example, a seed-rate-per-acre field on the product record). This
separation is what allows the system to answer “how much seed do I need for two acres?” safely
while refusing to answer “what should I spray on my diseased crop?” — the former is arithmetic
over a verified static field, the latter would require the LLM to diagnose a disease it cannot see and
has not been trained to safely diagnose.

Note on data provenance: illustrative examples in this proposal reference Indira Gandhi Krishi
Vishwavidyalaya (IGKV) as the intended authoritative source for regional crop and seed-rate metadata.
Specific variety names, seed-rate figures, and agronomic parameters have not yet been independently
sourced or verified by the team at proposal stage and must be obtained directly from IGKV
publications or extension materials during Phase 1 before being encoded into the product database; no
such figures are asserted as verified fact in this document.

15. Backend and API Design
The backend is implemented in Python using FastAPI, chosen for its native asynchronous request
handling, which matters because ASR/MT/LLM/TTS calls in the voice pipeline are all comparatively
slow, blocking operations relative to typical CRUD calls. The backend exposes a small, well-defined
REST/WebSocket surface: authentication, product search/detail endpoints, cart endpoints, an
order-simulation endpoint, and a streaming voice endpoint that internally sequences the ASR → MT
→ LLM → MT → TTS cascade described in Section 13.4. Server-Sent Events (or WebSockets) are
used to stream intermediate pipeline progress to the client so that the user receives feedback (e.g.
a brief audio cue) while the multi-second pipeline is still processing, rather than experiencing a
silent, unexplained delay.

Prompt-injection resistance is addressed structurally rather than through instruction alone: the LLM
is constrained to emit only predefined JSON tool-call schemas or short conversational text, so a
user attempting to instruct the model to “ignore previous instructions” cannot cause the backend
to execute an operation outside the fixed set of permitted tools.


16. Mobile Application
The mobile client is built in Flutter for cross-platform reach with a single codebase, and because
Flutter's audio-recording ecosystem (e.g. the record package) is mature enough for a three-person
team to integrate within the available time, compared with the steeper native-audio integration
effort that React Native or platform-native development would require. The client's responsibilities
are: capturing and locally VAD-gating microphone audio, compressing it (Opus codec) before upload
to minimise bandwidth use on constrained rural networks, rendering a minimal visual confirmation
of what was understood (recognised text, product images, running cart total) so the farmer is not
solely dependent on audio memory, and caching recently viewed product metadata and low-
resolution images locally so the app remains partially usable while a voice request is in flight or the
network is degraded.

17. AI/ML Development Strategy
The academic timeline rules out training a speech or language foundation model from scratch; the
AI/ML strategy is therefore built entirely around adapting and evaluating existing open models rather
than training new ones.

  • ASR and TTS: reuse and extend the team's already-trained MMS/VITS Chhattisgarhi models
     (Level 1 preliminary work), fine-tuning or prompt/vocabulary-biasing them toward agricultural
     and shopping vocabulary using the RESPIN and SYSPIN corpora described in Section 20.

  • Translation: use NLLB-200 directly, since it has verified native Chhattisgarhi support
     (hne_Deva); no fine-tuning is planned for the MVP, but translation-quality evaluation against
     the project's own evaluation set is planned (RQ2).

  • Intent/entity extraction: use prompt engineering and strict JSON-schema tool-calling over an
     existing instruction-tuned LLM rather than training a traditional intent classifier (e.g.
     Rasa/spaCy-style), because traditional classifiers require tens of thousands of annotated
     examples that do not exist for Chhattisgarhi agricultural commerce, whereas a general-
     purpose LLM can be steered with a comparatively small number of few-shot examples once
     the Chhattisgarhi query has been translated into English.

  • Recommendation logic: rule-based only, implemented in Python/SQL against verified product
     metadata, with no LLM involvement in any numeric agronomic calculation, consistent with
     Section 14.1.

  • Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is deliberately not used: the product catalogue is a
     small, structured relational dataset (well under a thousand items), for which direct SQL/full-
     text search is simpler, faster, and easier to audit than a RAG pipeline, and RAG's added
     architectural complexity is not justified at this scale.

18. Safety, Privacy and Security
18.1 Agricultural safety boundary (critical design requirement)
This system is designed, described, and evaluated strictly as a shopping and product-discovery
assistant. It is explicitly not designed, and must never be presented, as an autonomous agricultural
doctor or agronomic advisor. This distinction is treated as a first-class requirement of the
architecture rather than an afterthought, because an AI system that hallucinates a pesticide type
or dosage in response to a diagnostic question could cause real crop loss, financial harm, or health
and environmental risk.

The system prompt governing the LLM explicitly separates permitted and disallowed query
categories:

Permitted (retail / discovery) queries
  • “Show me urea fertiliser.” — catalogue search

  • “What seeds are available?” — catalogue browse

  • “Add two bags of fertiliser.” — cart operation

  • “How much seed is required for two acres?” — permitted only because it is answered by
     deterministic arithmetic over verified, static IGKV-sourced metadata, never by LLM inference

Disallowed (diagnostic / dosage) queries — trigger a safety refusal
  • “Which pesticide should I use for this disease?”

  • “What chemical will cure this crop?”

  • “How much pesticide should I apply to my diseased plants?”

When a disallowed query is detected, the LLM is constrained to emit a fixed, hardcoded referral
response directing the farmer to the local Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) rather than allowing free-form
generation, and the backend logs the refusal for later evaluation of the boundary's detection
accuracy (Objective 4, Section 7). This behaviour is treated as a core deliverable, evaluated with
the same rigor as the shopping functionality, and is discussed again under Risks (Section 26) and
Future Scope (Section 29), where any expansion toward personalised agronomic assistance is
explicitly deferred and marked as requiring qualified domain-expert and institutional oversight before
being considered.

19. Offline and Low-Connectivity Strategy
Fully offline Chhattisgarhi ASR on a low-end rural smartphone is not realistic within this project's
scope: even a comparatively compact ASR model requires more RAM and compute than is reliably
available on typical low-end Android hardware, and running the LLM reasoning step on-device is not
feasible for a lightweight Flutter application. The system therefore assumes a connected
architecture and instead focuses on graceful degradation rather than true offline operation.

  • Audio is compressed with the Opus codec before upload to minimise bandwidth demand on
     constrained 3G/4G rural networks.

  • Recently viewed product text and low-resolution images are cached locally on the client so
     the user can browse previously seen catalogue information even while a new voice request is
     in flight or the network is briefly unavailable.

  • The client enforces an 8-second request timeout; on timeout, the app aborts the pending
     request and plays a locally stored, pre-recorded Chhattisgarhi audio prompt explaining that
     the network is unavailable and inviting the user to try again, rather than freezing or failing
     silently.

21. Methodology
The project follows a phased, build-and-evaluate methodology rather than a single upfront design-
then-implement waterfall, because the team's confidence in several components (LLM tool-calling

in Section 22 produces a concrete, testable artefact, and the evaluation metrics defined in Section

be revisited before it blocks integration.

Objective-wise, the methodology maps as follows: Objective 1 (voice pipeline) is addressed through
Phases 1 and 3; Objective 2 (conversational agent) through Phase 3; Objective 3 (end-to-end mobile
prototype) through Phases 2, 4 and 5; and Objective 4 (safety boundary) through Phase 3 and the
dedicated safety-boundary evaluation in Phase 6.

29. Preliminary Work (Level 1 — Already Demonstrated)
Prior to this proposal, the team built and locally ran a working Chhattisgarhi speech-to-speech
pipeline. This section documents its technical specifics as evidence of feasibility for the voice-
interaction component of the Minor Project. No accuracy figures, benchmark numbers, or user-
testing results are claimed beyond what is stated below; where a formal metric has not yet been
calibrated, this is stated explicitly rather than estimated.

29.1 Speech-to-Text (STT) module
  • Built with FastAPI to handle live, concurrent, low-latency WebSocket connections streaming
     binary audio (16kHz PCM).

  • Uses Silero VAD for real-time voice activity detection, governing partial vs. final transcript
     emission and detecting user barge-in.

  • Implements a pluggable ASREngine interface with a shared inference thread pool, allowing
     multiple callers to be served without reloading the model.

  • Includes a telephony_adapter capable of intercepting 8kHz mu-law audio (e.g. from telephony
     networks such as Twilio), which is not required for the Minor Project's mobile-app scope but
     demonstrates the pipeline's underlying flexibility.

29.2 Text-to-Speech (TTS) module
  • Built on the Coqui-TTS library (TTS), synthesising Chhattisgarhi text strictly in Devanagari
     script.

  • Integrates speed-pacing control (length_scale) at the model level to adjust response pacing
     dynamically.

29.4 Measured and qualitative observations to date
  • STT inference speed: the MMS model used is a 1-billion-parameter model, substantially
     heavier than typical edge STT models; a benchmarking script (test_mms_speed.py) is in
     place to evaluate inference time against audio length to determine whether live partial
     transcripts are sustainable on target CPUs — this evaluation is ongoing and no final figure is
     claimed here.

  • TTS real-time factor: local notebook execution (indictts.ipynb) of the VITS model on GPU
     shows a processing time of approximately 117 ms and a real-time factor (RTF) of
     approximately 0.0138 for a standard sentence — i.e., synthesis completing roughly 72×
     faster than the resulting audio's playback duration. This is a local benchmark observation,
     not a formally published result.

  • Word Error Rate (WER): no calibrated WER benchmark on a standard test set has yet been
     produced for this pipeline; this is explicitly one of the evaluation tasks planned for Phase 6
     of this Minor Project (Section 24).

  • Devanagari CTC correction: the MMS model's raw CTC output occasionally inserts stray
     spaces before Devanagari matras, producing detached characters/dotted circles; a regex
     post-processor (clean_mms_devanagari) was built to correct this and materially improves
     output readability.

  • Domain vocabulary biasing: the pipeline supports initial-prompt biasing to reduce
     misrecognition of domain-specific proper nouns, previously applied in a different
     (clinical/appointment) context; this same mechanism will be repurposed for agricultural
     product and crop-name vocabulary in this Minor Project.

  • Barge-in handling: the pipeline emits a speech_started event carrying an
     interrupt_previous_response flag, allowing playback to be halted immediately when the user
     begins speaking again, supporting a more natural conversational flow.

29.5 What this preliminary work does and does not establish
This prior work demonstrates that real-time Chhattisgarhi ASR and TTS are achievable locally, on
student hardware, using entirely open-source models — the single technical claim this Minor Project
relies on most heavily from its preliminary work. It does not include any agricultural vocabulary
tuning, e-commerce logic, translation stage, LLM tool-calling, safety-boundary design, or formal
accuracy evaluation, all of which are new contributions of this Minor Project and are scoped and
evaluated as such throughout this document.

31. Conclusion
This proposal describes a scoped, technically grounded B.Tech Minor Project: a Chhattisgarhi voice-
first agricultural product-discovery and cart-management prototype, built primarily on open-source
components the team has already partially demonstrated, and constrained by an explicit, tested
safety boundary that keeps the AI in a retail-assistant role rather than an agronomic-advisor role.
The project deliberately separates what has already been demonstrated (Level 1), what will be built
and evaluated over the next sixteen weeks (Level 2, the MVP), and what might be pursued afterward
as a startup-oriented product direction (Level 3), so that the academic deliverable is never inflated
beyond what three students can responsibly build, test, and defend on a zero budget.

The team considers the project feasible specifically because its scope has been deliberately
narrowed — away from payments, live vendor logistics, and open-ended agronomic advisory AI —
toward a well-instrumented voice pipeline and a safety-bounded conversational shopping agent that
